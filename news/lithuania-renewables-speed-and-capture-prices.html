Analysis · Lithuania

Lithuania is adding renewables faster than it is adding the grid to carry them

Solar passed 3 GW in March. Wind and solar together already cover 68% of demand. Another 4 GW of solar holds technical permits. The binding constraint has quietly moved from megawatts to euros per megawatt-hour.

Boreal Grid Research · 16 September 2026 · 9 min read

The speed is real

Lithuania added roughly 600 MW of solar in 2025, taking installed capacity to 3,040 MW, and crossed the 3 GW mark in March 2026. On 15 May, Lithuanian solar plants hit an instantaneous 1,547 MW — 112 MW above the previous record of 1,435 MW set on 2 July 2025.

The generation mix in mid-2026 tells the same story from the other direction: wind 42%, solar 39%, thermal 9%, hydro 6%, other 4%. Wind and solar together are covering 68.1% of Lithuanian electricity demand. For a country that was a net importer of most of its power a decade ago, and that only desynchronised from the post-Soviet BRELL ring in February 2025, that is a genuinely fast transition by any European standard.

The target

Lithuania's National Energy Independence Strategy sets 100% of electricity from renewables by 2030. On build rate alone, that is not an obviously unreasonable target. On system integration, it is the hard part, and the rest of this piece is about why.

Prices fell — and then the question changed

Lithuanian day-ahead prices averaged €82.15/MWh in Q1 2026. Across late June and early July the average in the Lithuanian bidding zone fell about 20%, from roughly €100/MWh to €81/MWh, with wind doing most of the work.

A 20% fall in the wholesale price is unambiguously good for consumers and for industrial competitiveness. It is more complicated for the people financing the assets that caused it, because the number that matters to them is not the baseload average — it is the capture price, and the two diverge precisely when renewables succeed.

Every megawatt of solar Lithuania adds makes the next megawatt of solar slightly less bankable. That is not a reason to stop building. It is a reason to stop modelling revenue off the baseload curve.

The cannibalisation arithmetic

Because solar generates concentrated around midday — exactly when every other solar plant in the bidding zone is also generating — its capture price typically runs 15–40% below the baseload average. That gap is the cannibalisation effect, and it widens mechanically with penetration. When wind and solar output overlap, net load collapses and the price can go through zero.

Germany is the leading indicator here, and it is not a comfortable one. April solar capture factors fell from around 0.40 in 2025 to roughly 0.26 in 2026. The share of German solar generation produced during negative-price periods went from 32.6% to 46.8% year on year, and the longest continuous negative-price run stretched from seven hours to seventeen.

Read this carefully

Those capture-factor figures are German, not Lithuanian, and Lithuania's smaller, better-interconnected zone will not follow the same path at the same speed. We cite them because they show the shape of the curve at high penetration, not because they are a forecast for LT. Anyone quoting them as Lithuanian numbers is misusing them.

The practical consequence is visible in how projects are now being structured. Lithuanian developers are increasingly pairing solar with batteries — partly to improve grid connection prospects, partly to shift output into the evening peak, and partly because storage reduces curtailment risk where the local network cannot absorb midday output.

The grid is the actual constraint

Technical permits have already been issued for around 4 GW of additional solar. There is no serious prospect of the existing network absorbing that on current topology, and the connection-queue question is now the one that determines which projects are real.

Litgrid has tendered the Gižai substation, which will be Lithuania's largest and the pivot point for the Harmony Link interconnection, with completion scheduled for end-2030. Litgrid's published preliminary connection conditions set out the transmission expansion plan, and place the cost of infrastructure expansion on the tender winner.

Harmony Link itself has been rescoped: the 2025 agreement between Litgrid and PSE settled on a 700 MW land-based interconnection at 220 kV, rather than the original subsea concept. PSE has now approved the financial investment decision for onshore construction, complementing Litgrid's own commitment made in September. Completion is scheduled for end-2030.

That date matters. Until Harmony Link energises, Lithuania's access to continental European prices runs through LitPol and, for Nordic supply, through NordBalt and the EstLink corridor further north. As the Finnish EstLink 2 outage demonstrated, that corridor is thin enough that a single fault repriced the whole Baltic region by 41% in a week.

Offshore keeps not happening

Lithuania's second offshore wind tender, for a 700 MW site, was declared invalid and has been relaunched. Two failed processes in a row is a signal about the terms on offer rather than about the resource, and it should be read as one.

The more interesting offshore development is the Baltic–German PowerLink. Lithuania, Latvia and Germany have signed a declaration on a joint 2 GW interconnector that would open direct trade between the Baltics and Germany and provide a route to market for up to 2 GW of Lithuanian and Latvian offshore wind. Decisions on next steps were planned for the end of Q3 2026 — which is now.

What this means if you are deploying capital

  • Model the capture price, not the baseload curve. A solar project underwritten on the LT baseload average is mispriced by a margin that grows every year the pipeline delivers.
  • Treat the connection queue as the real pipeline. Four gigawatts of technical permits is not four gigawatts of projects; it is a filter that has not yet been applied.
  • Price storage as a revenue instrument, not as a grid-compliance cost. The evening-peak spread is where solar economics are being rebuilt.
  • Put a date and a probability on Harmony Link and PowerLink in your price deck. Post-2030 Lithuanian prices are a different distribution depending on whether both, one or neither is delivered.
  • Watch the Q3 2026 PowerLink decision. It is the nearest-term event that moves the long-dated Baltic curve.
