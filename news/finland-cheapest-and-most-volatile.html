Analysis · Finland

Finland is Europe's cheapest power market and its most volatile — for the same reason

Day-ahead averages have spent much of 2026 in the low teens per megawatt-hour. Balancing energy in the same country has cleared above €600/MWh on a monthly average. Those two facts are not in tension; they are the same fact seen from two different markets.

Boreal Grid Research · 15 September 2026 · 8 min read

The average is no longer the story

Finland has recorded day-ahead prices below €15/MWh through stretches of 2026, which puts it among the cheapest wholesale markets in Europe. On 5 September the time-weighted day-ahead average came in at 1.44 c/kWh — €14.40/MWh — with a low of 0.35 c/kWh in the late afternoon and a high of 3.90 c/kWh just after one in the morning.

Read that as a monthly average and Finland looks like a solved market: abundant, decarbonised, cheap. Read it as a distribution and it looks like something else entirely. Montel has been calling Finland Europe's most volatile short-term electricity market, and the balancing data from Fingrid supports the label rather than softening it.

The average price tells you what the system costs. The spread tells you what flexibility is worth. In Finland those two numbers have been moving in opposite directions for three years.

What changed on the supply side

Two things, and they compounded. Olkiluoto 3 came online in April 2023 with 1.6 GW of effectively must-run output. At the same time Finnish wind went from roughly 2 TWh in 2015 to more than 18 TWh in 2024 — close to a ninefold increase in under a decade, and enough to turn Finland into a structural exporter to the Baltic states.

A large inflexible block plus a large weather-driven block, in a system whose demand did not grow at anything like the same rate, produces exactly what has been observed: long periods of very low or zero-marginal-cost supply, punctuated by hours when the wind drops and the residual load has to be met from whatever is left. Finland recorded 467 negative-price hours in 2023, the most in Europe that year.

Worth noting

Negative prices have been conspicuously absent from recent windows — none of the 30 complete days to early September 2026 contained a negatively priced interval. That is a seasonal and hydrological result, not a structural fix. The underlying supply stack has not changed.

The volatility has moved into the balancing markets

This is the part that gets missed by anyone modelling Finland off day-ahead averages. Fingrid's aFRR energy activation market averaged €661/MWh in June. The move to 15-minute clearing for mFRR energy in March widened things further, with an average daily spread of €836/MWh.

The March overhaul also split Nordic balancing so that production and consumption are now balanced separately for each bidding zone. Alongside it, Fingrid cut its balance service fee from €1.35 to €1.05 per megawatt-hour with effect from 1 March 2026. The direction of travel is clear enough: cheaper to be balanced, far more expensive to be the one doing the balancing at the wrong moment.

A battery in Finland is not being paid for energy arbitrage in any meaningful sense. It is being paid for the option to be available in the intervals when the balancing market breaks three figures — and there are enough of those intervals now to underwrite a project.

Storage is the marginal asset, and it knows it

Roughly 1,050 MW of storage is installed in Finland, with close to 400 MW more under construction. Statkraft's 235 MW battery deal is the kind of transaction that follows from the numbers above rather than from a subsidy. Fingrid's own scenarios put the system's requirement at 1–4 GW of battery storage by 2035.

The gap between 1 GW installed and a 1–4 GW requirement is the whole investment case, and the wide upper bound is the whole risk. If the correct answer is 1 GW, Finland is close to built out and revenues compress. If it is 4 GW, the current fleet is a rounding error against what is needed. Nobody financing a Finnish battery in 2026 should be treating that range as a detail.

Why the Baltics should care more than they do

Finland's surplus does not stay in Finland. It moves south through EstLink, and the Baltic price level is partly a function of how much of it gets through. The December 2024 EstLink 2 failure — the cable was damaged when the tanker Eagle S is believed to have dragged its anchor across the seabed — is the cleanest natural experiment anyone could ask for.

With the link out, Baltic prices rose 41% week-on-week, from €56/MWh to €79/MWh. Repair costs ran to €50–60 million, split evenly between Elering and Fingrid, and the connection returned to the market on 15 July 2025.

A single cable fault repriced three national markets by 41% in a week. That is not a resilient system; that is a system with one dominant import corridor and a plan to build a second.

That episode is why our Lithuanian coverage treats interconnection as a price variable rather than as infrastructure trivia. The Baltic bidding zones do not clear on Baltic fundamentals alone.

What we are watching next

  • Whether the separated per-zone balancing design holds its spreads through a full winter, or whether March–June was a transition artefact.
  • The pace of battery commissioning against Fingrid's 1–4 GW range — the commissioning curve, not the pipeline, is the number that matters.
  • Whether industrial demand response shows up at scale now that the within-hour price signal is this sharp.
  • EstLink availability through the winter, and what the redundancy plan looks like in practice rather than on a slide.
